Part 0A linear algebra refresher

00Six ideas you'll need

Stereo vision is geometry, and the language of that geometry is linear algebra. Six ideas are enough to follow every step of this explainer. If you're comfortable with all of them, skip ahead to section 01. Otherwise, each takes a minute, and later sections point back here when they use one.

1 · Vectors, length and the dot product

A vector is a list of numbers, such as \(\mathbf a = (3, 4)\), which you can picture as an arrow. Its length is \(\lVert\mathbf a\rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\). A unit vector has length 1 and just indicates a direction.

The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them: \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + \dots\). It also equals \(\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors. So a dot product of zero means the vectors are perpendicular. Example: \((3,4)\cdot(4,-3) = 12 - 12 = 0\).

2 · A matrix is a transformation

Multiplying a vector by a matrix, \(A\mathbf x\), produces a new vector. Think of \(A\) as a machine that moves points around: it can rotate, stretch, shear or flatten space. A handy way to read any matrix is that its columns show where the axes end up.

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2x\\y\end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{(stretch by 2 along } x\text{)} \]

Applying two transformations in a row is a matrix product, \(B(A\mathbf x) = (BA)\mathbf x\). Mind the order: the matrix nearest the vector acts first. The transpose \(A^\top\) swaps rows and columns.

3 · Rotation matrices

A rotation matrix \(R\) turns vectors without changing their lengths or the angles between them. In 2D:

\[ R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}. \]

Its columns are perpendicular unit vectors. That gives a very useful shortcut: undoing a rotation is just the transpose, \(R^{-1} = R^\top\), because \(R^\top R = I\) (the identity matrix, which leaves every vector unchanged). A rotation also has determinant \(\det R = +1\). A matrix with perpendicular unit columns but determinant \(-1\) is a mirror reflection, which no real camera motion can produce. Section 05 has to guard against it.

In 3D, \(R\) is a 3×3 matrix with the same properties. A camera's orientation is a 3D rotation, and so is the relative turn between the two cameras of a stereo rig.

4 · The cross product, and writing it as a matrix

For 3D vectors, the cross product \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\) is a new vector perpendicular to both, with length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span. Example: \((1,0,0)\times(0,1,0) = (0,0,1)\). Two facts get used later:

  • \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf a = \mathbf 0\): a vector crossed with itself gives nothing.
  • Three vectors lie in one plane exactly when \(\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) = 0\). The vector \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\) sticks straight out of the plane of \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\), and \(\mathbf c\) is perpendicular to it only if \(\mathbf c\) lies in that plane. This "triple product" test is the whole idea behind the epipolar constraint in section 03.

Crossing with a fixed vector \(\mathbf t\) is a linear operation, so it can be written as an ordinary matrix multiplication:

\[ \mathbf t\times\mathbf a = [\mathbf t]_\times\,\mathbf a,\qquad [\mathbf t]_\times = \begin{bmatrix}0&-t_3&t_2\\t_3&0&-t_1\\-t_2&t_1&0\end{bmatrix}. \]

Multiply out \([\mathbf t]_\times\mathbf a\) and you get the usual cross-product formula. This small matrix sits inside the essential matrix.

5 · Rank, flattening and \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0\)

Some matrices flatten space: they squash a whole direction down to zero. For example

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

The second row is just twice the first, so the matrix only carries one row's worth of information. Its rank (the number of independent directions it can output) is 1 rather than 2, its determinant is 0, and it cannot be undone. Such a matrix is called singular, and the directions it sends to zero form its null space.

Many steps in this explainer come down to the same task: find a nonzero \(\mathbf x\) with \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0\), that is, find the direction \(A\) flattens. The SVD (section 02) answers it, and also gives the best compromise when noise means no \(\mathbf x\) makes \(A\mathbf x\) exactly zero.

6 · Homogeneous coordinates and "up to scale"

A trick used throughout computer vision is to write a 2D image point \((x, y)\) as the 3-vector \((x, y, 1)\). Any multiple, such as \((2x, 2y, 2)\), stands for the same point; to get back, divide by the last entry. This suits cameras, because a pixel really represents a ray from the camera, and every point along a ray has the same image.

There are two payoffs. First, perspective projection (dividing by depth) and shifting points (adding \(\mathbf t\)) both become plain matrix multiplications. Second, equations of the form \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0\) only ever pin \(\mathbf x\) down up to scale: if \(\mathbf x\) is a solution, so is \(5\mathbf x\), so we usually pick the solution of length 1. That's why the essential matrix and the translation direction are only known up to scale, and why a stereo rig needs its baseline measured to give distances in metres.

Part IOne camera, and why it can't see depth

01Pinhole image formation

An ideal camera is a pinhole: every scene point sends one ray through the optical centre, and the image is where those rays cross a plane at distance \(f\) (the focal length). Put the camera at the origin, looking down its \(Z\) axis. By similar triangles a point \((X, Y, Z)\) lands at

\[ x = f\,\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\,\frac{Y}{Z}. \]

The division by \(Z\) is perspective: far things look small. It is also where depth goes missing. Scale \((X,Y,Z)\) by any \(\lambda>0\) and the image point does not move. A single image fixes the ray through each pixel, but not where along the ray the surface is.

The full camera model

Real cameras measure in pixels rather than millimetres, and the camera usually isn't sitting at the world origin. Both corrections are matrix multiplications, and homogeneous coordinates (section 00) chain them into one formula. Read it right to left. A world point, with a 1 appended, is moved into the camera's frame by \([R\,|\,\mathbf t]\), then converted to pixels by \(K\). The left side is only known up to a scale factor \(\lambda\), which is the point's depth: that's the lost depth again.

\[ \lambda\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & c_x\\ 0 & f_y & c_y\\ 0&0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{K\ \text{(intrinsics)}}\;\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} R & \mathbf t\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{extrinsics}}\begin{bmatrix}X_w\\Y_w\\Z_w\\1\end{bmatrix} = P\,\mathbf X_w . \]
  • Extrinsics \([R\,|\,\mathbf t]\) move a world point into the camera frame. For a stereo rig, the extrinsics between the two cameras are the quantity sections 03–05 are about.
  • Intrinsics \(K\) hold the focal lengths in pixels \(f_x, f_y\), the principal point \((c_x, c_y)\) and a skew \(s\) that is zero on modern sensors.
  • Normalised coordinates \(\mathbf x = K^{-1}(u, v, 1)^\top\) undo the intrinsics. They are ray directions with \(z = 1\), and the two-view geometry is cleanest in them.

Real lenses also bend rays, so straight lines in the world look curved, especially near the image edges. Calibration measures this distortion so it can be removed.

Going deeper: the lens distortion model

The Brown–Conrady model used by OpenCV adds radial terms \(k_1, k_2, k_3\) and tangential terms \(p_1, p_2\) to normalised coordinates, with \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\):

\[ x_d = x\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1xy + p_2(r^2 + 2x^2) \]
\[ y_d = y\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + p_1(r^2 + 2y^2) + 2p_2xy \]

\(K\) and the distortion coefficients come from calibration, usually Zhang's method with a checkerboard seen in several poses. Everything that follows assumes images have been undistorted, so that straight lines stay straight and the pinhole equations hold.

The plan. One camera gives a ray per pixel. Two cameras give two rays, and where they cross is the 3D point. To use that, we need (1) the geometry linking the two cameras, (2) a cheap way to find which pixel in one image matches which in the other, and (3) the formula from that match to depth. The SVD is the tool used throughout, and Part II covers it. If matrices, cross products or "up to scale" feel rusty, section 00 is a short refresher.

Part IIThe tool used throughout

02The singular value decomposition

Almost every step from here to a depth map ends in the same move: a small matrix problem solved with the SVD. Estimating the essential matrix, cleaning it up, splitting it into rotation and translation, and triangulating points all use it. This section builds the intuition once.

In plain words. Any matrix, however messy, does just three simple things to a vector: it rotates it, stretches it along the axes, then rotates it again. The SVD tells you exactly which rotations and how much stretch. The stretch factors, called singular values, say how strongly the matrix acts in each direction. A stretch of zero means some direction is flattened away completely.

As a formula, every matrix \(A\) factors as

\[ A = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top \]
  • \(V^\top\) is a rotation. The columns \(\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2, \dots\) of \(V\) are special input directions, the right singular vectors.
  • \(\Sigma\) is diagonal: it stretches along the axes by \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\), the singular values, sorted largest first.
  • \(U\) is another rotation. Its columns \(\mathbf u_1, \mathbf u_2, \dots\) are the matching output directions, the left singular vectors.

Put together, \(A\mathbf v_i = \sigma_i\mathbf u_i\): feed in the special direction \(\mathbf v_i\) and you get out the direction \(\mathbf u_i\), stretched by \(\sigma_i\). In 2D this means a circle always becomes an ellipse, with axes \(\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\) and \(\sigma_2\mathbf u_2\). (Strictly, \(U\) or \(V\) may also include a mirror flip; see rotation matrices in section 00.)

Three SVD facts that do all the work

You don't need to know how the SVD is computed: every numerical library has it (np.linalg.svd). You do need these three facts, because each one is a step in the stereo pipeline.

① Best answer to \(A\mathbf x \approx \mathbf 0\)

Among all vectors of length 1, the one \(A\) shrinks the most is the last right singular vector \(\mathbf v_n\). It shrinks by \(\sigma_n\), the smallest singular value. If \(\sigma_n = 0\), then \(A\mathbf v_n = \mathbf 0\) exactly. Used for: estimating E (§04) and triangulating points (§08).

② Nearest cleaned-up matrix

Change the singular values but keep \(U\) and \(V\), and you get the closest matrix that has the new singular values. Setting the smallest to zero gives the closest matrix that flattens a direction. Used for: turning a noisy E into a valid one (§04).

③ The flattened directions

If \(\sigma_n = 0\), then \(\mathbf v_n\) is the input direction \(A\) flattens to zero, and \(\mathbf u_n\) is the output direction \(A\) can never produce. Used for: finding the epipoles and the translation direction (§05).

Where does the SVD come from? Here is a five-step construction that shows every matrix has one. The key question is which input directions does \(A\) keep perpendicular? Those are the \(\mathbf v_i\).

  1. Look at \(A^\top A\). It is square and symmetric (it equals its own transpose). It also never gives a negative "energy": \(\mathbf x^\top A^\top A\mathbf x = \lVert A\mathbf x\rVert^2 \ge 0\).
  2. Take its eigenvectors. A standard theorem (the spectral theorem) says a symmetric matrix has a full set of perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2, \dots\), with \(A^\top A\,\mathbf v_i = \lambda_i\mathbf v_i\). By step 1 the eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda_i \ge 0\). Sort them largest first.
  3. Read off the stretch. \(\lVert A\mathbf v_i\rVert^2 = \mathbf v_i^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf v_i = \lambda_i\), so \(A\) stretches \(\mathbf v_i\) by \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\). Those are the singular values.
  4. Find where each direction goes. Set \(\mathbf u_i = A\mathbf v_i / \sigma_i\), a unit vector. These outputs are perpendicular too: for \(i \ne j\), \(\ \mathbf u_i\cdot\mathbf u_j = \mathbf v_i^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf v_j / (\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \lambda_j\,(\mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j)/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = 0\). So \(A\) maps one set of perpendicular directions onto another set of perpendicular directions. (If some \(\sigma_i = 0\), complete the \(\mathbf u\)'s with any perpendicular unit vectors.)
  5. Stack the columns. \(A\mathbf v_i = \sigma_i\mathbf u_i\) for every \(i\) reads \(AV = U\Sigma\) in matrix form. \(V\) is a rotation, so \(V^{-1} = V^\top\) (section 00), and multiplying both sides by \(V^\top\) gives \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\). ∎

Worked example. \(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}\), with eigenvalues 45 and 5 and eigenvectors \(\mathbf v_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\), \(\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\). So \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} \approx 6.71\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24\). Then \(\mathbf u_1 = A\mathbf v_1/\sigma_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3)\) and \(\mathbf u_2 = A\mathbf v_2/\sigma_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3, -1)\), which are perpendicular, as promised. To see it in the playground above (its sliders stop at ±2), enter \(A/5\): \(a_{11} = 0.6,\ a_{12} = 0,\ a_{21} = 0.8,\ a_{22} = 1\). The directions are the same and the singular values are divided by 5, giving 1.34 and 0.45.

Libraries such as np.linalg.svd don't actually form \(A^\top A\), because squaring the matrix squares its rounding errors. They use more stable algorithms (Golub–Kahan bidiagonalisation) that give the same factors. For the tiny 3×3 and 9×9 matrices in stereo, the route above is accurate enough, and it is what the demos on this page use. The same reasoning applied to \(AA^\top\) shows that its eigenvectors are the \(\mathbf u_i\).

Why "singular"?

Here "singular" has its older meaning of special or distinguished. It does not mean "single", and a singular value is not the same thing as a singular matrix. The name comes from early-1900s work on integral equations. It belongs to the same family as eigenvalue (German eigen, "own" or "characteristic"): a number that belongs to the operator and describes how it acts. G. W. Stewart's On the Early History of the Singular Value Decomposition (1993) traces where the term came from.

Singular values are still closely tied to singular, non-invertible matrices:

  • \(\sigma\) is a singular value of \(A\) exactly when \(A^\top A - \sigma^2 I\) is singular. The singular values are the values of \(\sigma\) that make that matrix singular.
  • \(A\) is singular exactly when its smallest singular value is zero. The matching \(\mathbf v_n\) is then the direction that \(A\) sends to \(\mathbf 0\). You can see this in the playground above by dragging \(a_{22}\) until \(\det A = 0\).
  • \(\sigma_{\min}\) is the distance, in the 2-norm, from \(A\) to the nearest singular matrix. A small \(\sigma_{\min}\) means \(A\) is nearly singular and badly conditioned.

Both meanings in this pipeline. With noise-free matches, the data matrix \(A\) is singular: \(A\mathbf e = \mathbf 0\) has a nonzero solution, the right singular vector for \(\sigma_9 = 0\). The essential matrix must also be singular, since rank 2 means \(\sigma_3 = 0\). Noise makes the estimate \(\hat E\) invertible, and setting \(\sigma_3 = 0\) (section 04) snaps it back to the nearest singular matrix. The directions \(E\) then sends to zero, \(\mathbf v_3\) and \(\mathbf u_3\), are the two epipoles, and \(\mathbf u_3\) is also the direction of \(\mathbf t\).

Part IIITwo views: the geometry linking the cameras

03Epipolar geometry and the essential matrix

In plain words. A pixel in the left image is a ray in 3D. Seen from the right camera, that ray appears as a line. So the matching pixel can't be just anywhere: it must lie on that line. The essential matrix \(E\) is a 3×3 matrix that takes a left pixel and gives you that line. It is built entirely from how the two cameras are positioned relative to each other.

Take two calibrated cameras, so we know their \(K\) and can work in normalised coordinates \(\mathbf x = K^{-1}\mathbf p\): each image point becomes a ray direction \((x, y, 1)\). Describe camera 2's pose relative to camera 1 by a rotation \(R\) and a translation \(\mathbf t\), so a 3D point with coordinates \(\mathbf X\) in camera 1 has coordinates \(\mathbf X' = R\mathbf X + \mathbf t\) in camera 2.

The two camera centres and \(\mathbf X\) define a plane, the epipolar plane. Its intersection with each image is an epipolar line, and all epipolar lines in one image pass through the epipole, the image of the other camera's centre.

Deriving \(E\) from coplanarity

Express everything in camera 2's frame. Three vectors lie in the epipolar plane: the ray to the point in image 2, \(\mathbf x'\); the baseline \(\mathbf t\) (camera 1's centre, since \(\mathbf X=\mathbf 0\mapsto\mathbf t\)); and the ray from camera 1, rotated into frame 2, \(R\mathbf x\). Three coplanar vectors have zero triple product:

Worked example. Take two cameras facing the same way (\(R = I\), no rotation), with camera 2 one metre to the right of camera 1. Seen from camera 2, camera 1's centre is one metre to the left, so \(\mathbf t = (-1, 0, 0)\). Put a point at \(\mathbf X = (0.5,\ 0.4,\ 2)\) in camera 1's frame.

  • Left image: \(\mathbf x = \mathbf X / 2 = (0.25,\ 0.2,\ 1)\), so \(\lambda = 2\).
  • In camera 2: \(\mathbf X' = \mathbf X + \mathbf t = (-0.5,\ 0.4,\ 2)\), so \(\mathbf x' = (-0.25,\ 0.2,\ 1)\) and \(\mu = 2\).
  • Two routes: \(\lambda R\mathbf x + \mathbf t = (0.5, 0.4, 2) + (-1, 0, 0) = (-0.5, 0.4, 2) = \mu\mathbf x'\). ✓
  • Plane normal: \(\mathbf t\times R\mathbf x = (-1,0,0)\times(0.25,0.2,1) = (0,\ 1,\ -0.2)\).
  • Test: \(\mathbf x'\cdot(0, 1, -0.2) = 0 + 0.2 - 0.2 = 0\). ✓ The match lies in the epipolar plane.

Also note that \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf x'\) have the same \(y\) value, 0.2. With parallel cameras side by side, a match always sits on the same row. Section 06 (rectification) makes any stereo pair behave like this.

That is the triple-product test from section 00: three vectors in one plane. Now write the cross product as a matrix, \(\mathbf t\times\mathbf a = [\mathbf t]_\times\mathbf a\), and the condition becomes a single matrix equation:

\[ [\mathbf t]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -t_3 & t_2\\ t_3 & 0 & -t_1\\ -t_2 & t_1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boxed{\;\mathbf x'^\top E\,\mathbf x = 0,\qquad E = [\mathbf t]_\times R\;} \]

This is the epipolar constraint (Longuet-Higgins, 1981). It is bilinear in the two image points and linear in the nine entries of \(E\), which is what makes linear estimation possible.

What makes a matrix "essential"

Two properties of \(E\) matter later, and both follow from \(E = [\mathbf t]_\times R\):

  • E flattens one direction. \([\mathbf t]_\times\) sends \(\mathbf t\) itself to zero, because \(\mathbf t\times\mathbf t = \mathbf 0\). So \(E\) is singular (rank 2), and its smallest singular value is 0.
  • Its other two singular values are equal. The rotation \(R\) doesn't stretch anything, so all of \(E\)'s stretching comes from \([\mathbf t]_\times\). That matrix stretches every direction perpendicular to \(\mathbf t\) by the same amount, \(\lVert\mathbf t\rVert\).

Key fact. The singular values of every essential matrix have the pattern \((\sigma, \sigma, 0)\). Any 3×3 matrix with that pattern is a valid essential matrix.

Going deeper: the formal statement

Huang & Faugeras (1989): a real \(3\times3\) matrix is an essential matrix if and only if its singular values are \((\sigma, \sigma, 0)\) with \(\sigma > 0\). Equivalently, \(\det E = 0\) and \(2EE^\top E - \operatorname{tr}(EE^\top)\,E = 0\). These cubic constraints are what Nistér's 5-point solver uses directly. The singular values of \([\mathbf t]_\times\) are \((\lVert\mathbf t\rVert, \lVert\mathbf t\rVert, 0)\) because it is skew-symmetric with eigenvalues \(0, \pm i\lVert\mathbf t\rVert\), and multiplying by an orthogonal \(R\) leaves singular values unchanged.

Because \(E\) is only defined up to scale (doubling \(E\) still gives \(\mathbf x'^\top E\mathbf x = 0\), and images alone cannot tell a large distant scene from a small near one), it has 5 degrees of freedom: 3 for rotation and 2 for the translation direction. The SVD is the natural language here, because the defining property of \(E\) is a statement about its singular values.

For uncalibrated cameras, the same constraint holds in pixels with the fundamental matrix \(F = K'^{-\top} E K^{-1}\), which only needs rank 2 (7 DoF). The machinery below is the same except for the final projection step.

04Estimating E: the 8-point algorithm

For a stereo rig, \(R\) and \(\mathbf t\) are the extrinsics between the two cameras, the one geometric fact the rest of the pipeline needs. They are normally measured once with a calibration target: OpenCV's stereoCalibrate minimises checkerboard reprojection error and returns \(R\), \(\mathbf t\), \(E\) and \(F\) together. Estimating \(E\) from ordinary scene matches is what you do when there is no target. That covers structure from motion and visual odometry, and also online recalibration of a rig whose extrinsics have drifted. It is also the clearest view of what calibration recovers.

In plain words. Each matched pair of points gives one equation that \(E\) must satisfy. \(E\) has 9 numbers, but its overall scale doesn't matter, so 8 matches are enough to pin it down. Stack the equations, and the SVD finds the \(E\) that comes closest to satisfying all of them. Noise means that \(E\) won't be quite valid, so a second SVD tidies it up.

Expand \(\mathbf x'^\top E\mathbf x = 0\) for one match \(\mathbf x=(x,y,1)\), \(\mathbf x'=(x',y',1)\). With \(\mathbf e\) the 9 entries of \(E\) read row by row:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x'x & x'y & x' & y'x & y'y & y' & x & y & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{(\mathbf x'\otimes\mathbf x)^\top}\;\mathbf e = 0 . \]

For example, the match \(\mathbf x = (0.1, 0.2, 1)\), \(\mathbf x' = (0.15, 0.18, 1)\) gives the row \((0.015,\ 0.03,\ 0.15,\ 0.018,\ 0.036,\ 0.18,\ 0.1,\ 0.2,\ 1)\). Every match contributes one row of known numbers, and the nine entries of \(E\) are the unknowns.

Stack one row per match to get a system \(A\mathbf e = \mathbf 0\), where \(A\) has \(n\) rows and 9 columns. There are two snags:

  • \(\mathbf e = \mathbf 0\) always solves it, which is useless. So we insist that \(\mathbf e\) has length 1. That costs nothing, because the scale of \(E\) doesn't matter anyway ("up to scale", section 00).
  • With noisy matches no \(\mathbf e\) makes every row exactly zero. So we look for the one that makes \(A\mathbf e\) as small as possible.

That is exactly SVD fact ①: the answer is \(\mathbf v_9\), the last right singular vector of \(A\). Reshape its nine numbers into a 3×3 matrix and you have the estimate \(\hat E\) (the hat means "estimated").

Snapping onto the essential space

\(\hat E\) was found without being told what a valid \(E\) looks like. With noisy data its singular values come out as something like \((1.05, 0.95, 0.08)\) rather than the required \((\sigma, \sigma, 0)\). The fix is SVD fact ②: take the SVD \(\hat E = U\,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3)\,V^\top\), keep \(U\) and \(V\), and overwrite the singular values with the correct pattern.

\[ \tilde E = U\,\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)\,V^\top \qquad\Big(\text{or } \operatorname{diag}\big(\tfrac{\sigma_1+\sigma_2}{2}, \tfrac{\sigma_1+\sigma_2}{2}, 0\big)\Big) \]

\(\tilde E\) is the valid essential matrix closest to \(\hat E\), measured entry by entry (the Frobenius norm). Because the overall scale of \(E\) is arbitrary, \(\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)\) is the usual choice. A visible side effect: with rank 3, the epipolar lines in image 2 have no common point. After projection they all pass through the epipole \(\mathbf e' \propto \mathbf u_3\).

What is the Frobenius norm? It measures the size of a matrix the same way you measure the length of a vector: square every entry, add them all up, and take the square root.

\[ \lVert A\rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2}, \qquad \text{e.g.}\quad \left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert_F = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4 + 16} = 5. \]

Think of it as unrolling the matrix into one long vector (9 numbers for a 3×3) and taking its ordinary length. The distance between two matrices is then \(\lVert A - B\rVert_F\), the "size" of their entry-by-entry difference. So "the closest valid \(E\)" means the valid matrix whose nine entries differ least from \(\hat E\)'s, in this total-squared-difference sense.

It links neatly to the SVD: the Frobenius norm also equals \(\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \dots}\). In the example above that's \(\sqrt{5^2 + 0^2} = 5\), the same answer. Rotations don't change it, so when two matrices share \(U\) and \(V\), their distance depends only on how their singular values differ. That is why simply overwriting the singular values gives the closest match. For \(\hat E\) with singular values \((1.05, 0.95, 0.08)\), the distance to \(\tilde E\) is \(\sqrt{0.05^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.08^2} \approx 0.11\).

Practical tip: rescale before you solve. In pixel units the columns of \(A\) mix huge and tiny numbers (\(x'x\) can be around 100,000 while the last column is 1), which makes the SVD answer very sensitive to noise. Hartley (1997) gave the fix: shift each image's points so their average sits at the origin, scale them so their average distance from it is \(\sqrt2\), solve, then undo the scaling with \(E = T'^\top \tilde E_n T\) and clean up again. Normalised camera coordinates are already well scaled, which is why the code on this page skips this step.

05Recovering R and t from E

In plain words. \(E\) was built as "translation-cross times rotation". Now we run that backwards. The SVD of \(E\) hands us both pieces almost directly, apart from two sign choices, which leave four candidate answers. A simple physical test then picks the right one.

The recipe:

  1. Take the SVD \(\tilde E = U\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)V^\top\). If \(\det U\) or \(\det V\) is \(-1\), multiply that matrix by \(-1\). This keeps them proper rotations rather than mirror flips (section 00) and doesn't change \(\tilde E\) except possibly its sign, which doesn't matter.
  2. Translation: \(\mathbf t = \pm\mathbf u_3\), the last column of \(U\). This is SVD fact ③: \(\mathbf t\) is the direction that gets flattened, since \(\mathbf t\times\mathbf t = \mathbf 0\). Its length can't be recovered, so we set it to 1.
  3. Rotation: \(R = UWV^\top\) or \(R = UW^\top V^\top\), where \(W\) is a fixed matrix, a 90° turn about the \(z\)-axis:
\[ W = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad R \in \{\,UWV^\top,\; UW^\top V^\top\,\},\qquad \mathbf t \in \{\,+\mathbf u_3,\; -\mathbf u_3\,\}. \]
Going deeper: why the matrix W appears

Define \(Z = \begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\), which is skew-symmetric, and note that \(ZW = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)\). Then

\[ \tilde E = U\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)V^\top = (UZU^\top)(UWV^\top). \]

The first factor is skew-symmetric, so it is \([\mathbf t]_\times\) for some \(\mathbf t\) (and that \(\mathbf t\) is \(\pm\mathbf u_3\)). The second is a rotation. That is exactly the form \([\mathbf t]_\times R\). Working through the other ways of splitting the product gives the alternative \(UW^\top V^\top\) (Hartley & Zisserman, Result 9.19).

Four candidates, one physical answer

All four \((R,\mathbf t)\) pairs satisfy the epipolar constraint equally well. They differ in where the triangulated points land. Only one places the scene in front of both cameras (positive depth, or cheirality).

Part IVFrom geometry to depth

06Rectification

The epipolar constraint already turns matching from a 2D search into a 1D one: the match for \(\mathbf x\) lies on the line \(\mathbf l' = F\mathbf x\). But those lines are slanted, differ for every pixel and converge on the epipole, which makes dense matching awkward and slow. Rectification warps both images so that every epipolar line becomes the same horizontal row in both views. Matching then becomes a scan along \(x\) at fixed \(y\).

How it works

Turning a camera on the spot, without moving it, doesn't change what it can see, only how the picture is laid out. The old and new images are related by a fixed 3×3 warp called a homography, and computing it needs no depth. So we can virtually turn both cameras into an ideal pose: facing the same way, with their \(x\) axes along the line joining them. With \(\mathbf X' = R\mathbf X + \mathbf t\), camera 2's centre in camera 1's frame is \(\mathbf c = -R^\top\mathbf t\). Build a new orientation whose \(x\) axis points along the baseline:

\[ \mathbf r_1 = \frac{\mathbf c}{\lVert\mathbf c\rVert},\qquad \mathbf r_2 = \frac{\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r_1}{\lVert\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r_1\rVert},\qquad \mathbf r_3 = \mathbf r_1\times\mathbf r_2,\qquad R_{\text{rect}} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf r_1^\top\\ \mathbf r_2^\top\\ \mathbf r_3^\top\end{bmatrix}. \]

In words: \(\mathbf r_1\) points along the baseline. \(\mathbf r_2\) is perpendicular to both the baseline and the old viewing direction \(\hat{\mathbf z}\), which is exactly what a cross product gives. \(\mathbf r_3\), the new viewing direction, is perpendicular to both. Three perpendicular unit vectors stacked as rows make a rotation matrix (section 00). Each image is then warped by its homography, with one shared intrinsic matrix \(K_n\):

\[ H_L = K_n\,R_{\text{rect}}\,K_L^{-1}, \qquad H_R = K_n\,R_{\text{rect}}\,R^\top K_R^{-1}. \]

In the rectified frame the cameras differ only by a shift \(B\) along \(x\), so \(\mathbf t = (-B, 0, 0)\) and the epipolar constraint collapses to

\[ \mathbf x'^\top\,[\mathbf t]_\times\,\mathbf x = B\,(y' - y) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y' = y . \]

The epipoles have moved to infinity along \(x\), and matched points share a row.

In practice, OpenCV's stereoRectify uses Bouguet's variant, which splits the rotation so each camera turns half way. That keeps the warps, and the resampling blur, as small as possible. initUndistortRectifyMap then folds undistortion and rectification into a single lookup table per camera, applied every frame with remap. Errors in \(R\) and \(\mathbf t\) show up here as vertical misalignment between rows. Even a pixel of it degrades matching, which is why calibration quality matters so much in stereo.

07Correspondence and disparity

After rectification, a pixel at \((x, y)\) in the left image matches some \((x - d, y)\) in the right, where \(d \ge 0\) is the disparity. Finding \(d\) for every pixel produces a disparity map. Most methods follow the same four steps (Scharstein & Szeliski's taxonomy):

  1. Matching cost \(C(x, y, d)\): how different the left pixel looks from the right pixel \(d\) to the left. Common choices are absolute difference, normalised cross-correlation (robust to gain and offset changes between cameras) and the census transform (compares each pixel only to its neighbours, so it ignores brightness differences).
  2. Aggregation: sum the cost over a window, since a single pixel is ambiguous. Big windows are less noisy but blur depth edges.
  3. Optimisation: winner-takes-all simply picks the disparity with the lowest cost at each pixel. Semi-global matching (SGM) also prefers neighbouring pixels to have similar disparities: small changes pay a small penalty \(P_1\), big jumps a larger penalty \(P_2\). It is the default dense method in OpenCV (StereoSGBM).
  4. Refinement: a left-right consistency check (match right-to-left too and reject pixels that disagree, which removes most occlusions), sub-pixel interpolation by fitting a parabola through the costs at \(d-1, d, d+1\), and speckle filtering.
Going deeper: the SGM recurrence

Along each of 8 or 16 scan directions \(\mathbf r\), SGM accumulates a path cost

\[ L_r(\mathbf p, d) = C(\mathbf p, d) + \min\big(L_r(\mathbf p - \mathbf r, d),\; L_r(\mathbf p - \mathbf r, d\pm1) + P_1,\; \min_k L_r(\mathbf p - \mathbf r, k) + P_2\big) - \min_k L_r(\mathbf p - \mathbf r, k). \]

The path costs are summed over directions and the disparity with the lowest total is chosen. The final subtraction just stops the numbers growing along the path. Hirschmüller (2008) gives the details.

The cost volume \(C(x,y,d)\) is the central data structure. Learned stereo networks build and filter the same volume, with features and aggregation learned from data rather than hand-designed.

08Depth from disparity

With a rectified pair the triangulation is just similar triangles. Both image planes sit at distance \(f\) in front of the camera centres, which are a baseline \(B\) apart.

\[ \boxed{\;Z = \frac{f\,B}{d}\;},\qquad X = \frac{(x - c_x)\,Z}{f},\qquad Y = \frac{(y - c_y)\,Z}{f}. \]

With \(f\) in pixels and \(B\) in metres, \(Z\) comes out in metres. Example: \(f = 800\) px, \(B = 0.12\) m and a measured disparity of \(d = 24\) px give \(Z = 800 \times 0.12 / 24 = 4\) m. Depth is inversely proportional to disparity: halve the disparity and the depth doubles. That one fact drives everything in the next section.

Going deeper: the reprojection matrix Q

The three equations above are one \(4\times4\) homogeneous map, which stereoRectify returns as \(Q\) and reprojectImageTo3D applies to the whole disparity image. For rectified cameras with a common principal point and right camera at \(T_x = -B\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\W\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&-c_x\\0&1&0&-c_y\\0&0&0&f\\0&0&-1/T_x&0\end{bmatrix}}_{Q}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\d\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad (X, Y, Z)/W . \]

Here \(W = d/B\), so \(Z/W = fB/d\) as required. (If the rectified principal points differ, the last entry becomes \((c_x - c_x')/T_x\).)

Going deeper: triangulating when the images are not rectified

For a general pair \(P = K[I\,|\,\mathbf 0]\), \(P' = K'[R\,|\,\mathbf t]\), the two rays rarely meet exactly because of noise. The standard linear method writes \(\mathbf x\times P\mathbf X = \mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf x'\times P'\mathbf X = \mathbf 0\), stacks two rows from each view into a \(4\times4\) matrix \(M\), and solves \(\min_{\lVert\mathbf X\rVert=1}\lVert M\mathbf X\rVert\). That is SVD fact ① again, the same routine the cheirality test in section 05 uses. Rectification turns this into the closed form above for every pixel at once.

09How accurate is stereo depth?

Differentiate \(Z = fB/d\) with respect to \(d\) and substitute \(d = fB/Z\):

\[ \left|\frac{\partial Z}{\partial d}\right| = \frac{fB}{d^2} = \frac{Z^2}{fB} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{f\,B}\,\delta d . \]

Example: the 12 cm, 800 px rig above has \(fB = 96\) px·m. With a matching error of a quarter pixel, the error at 4 m is about \(16 \times 0.25 / 96 \approx 4\) cm. At 20 m it is about \(400 \times 0.25 / 96 \approx 1\) m.

So depth error grows with the square of range. Doubling the distance quadruples the error, and relative error \(\delta Z / Z\) grows linearly. The levers are:

  • Baseline \(B\): error falls as \(1/B\). Wider rigs see further but have a larger minimum range \(Z_{\min} = fB/d_{\max}\) and more occlusion, and are harder to keep calibrated.
  • Focal length \(f\) in pixels: more resolution or a narrower field of view gives a larger \(f\). Doubling sensor width at the same field of view halves the error, at four times the pixels.
  • Matching precision \(\delta d\): sub-pixel interpolation reaches a fraction of a pixel on well-textured surfaces. That precision assumes rectification is accurate to a similar level.

The linear formula is a first-order approximation. The true interval is asymmetric: an error of \(-\delta d\) moves the estimate further away than \(+\delta d\) brings it closer, and the gap widens as \(d\) approaches \(\delta d\).

In OpenCV

import numpy as np

# 1. Stereo calibration from checkerboard corners seen by both cameras (intrinsics already known).
#    R, T map points from the left camera frame to the right: X_R = R X_L + T. E = [T]x R.
rms, K1, D1, K2, D2, R, T, E, F = cv2.stereoCalibrate(
    obj_pts, img_pts_left, img_pts_right, K1, D1, K2, D2, image_size,
    flags=cv2.CALIB_FIX_INTRINSIC)

# 2. Rectification: rotations R1, R2, new projections P1, P2 and the reprojection matrix Q.
R1, R2, P1, P2, Q, roi1, roi2 = cv2.stereoRectify(K1, D1, K2, D2, image_size, R, T, alpha=0)
map_lx, map_ly = cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap(K1, D1, R1, P1, image_size, cv2.CV_32FC1)
map_rx, map_ry = cv2.initUndistortRectifyMap(K2, D2, R2, P2, image_size, cv2.CV_32FC1)

# --- per frame ---
rect_l = cv2.remap(img_left, map_lx, map_ly, cv2.INTER_LINEAR)
rect_r = cv2.remap(img_right, map_rx, map_ry, cv2.INTER_LINEAR)

# 3. Semi-global matching. P1/P2 are the small/large disparity-change penalties.
block = 5
sgbm = cv2.StereoSGBM_create(minDisparity=0, numDisparities=128, blockSize=block,
                             P1=8 * 3 * block**2, P2=32 * 3 * block**2,
                             uniquenessRatio=10, speckleWindowSize=100, speckleRange=2,
                             mode=cv2.STEREO_SGBM_MODE_SGBM_3WAY)
disp = sgbm.compute(rect_l, rect_r).astype(np.float32) / 16.0   # fixed point, 4 fractional bits

# 4. Depth and point cloud (units of T).
points = cv2.reprojectImageTo3D(disp, Q)
valid = disp > 0
depth = points[..., 2][valid]

Estimating E yourself

A compact NumPy version of sections 04–05, for natural-feature matches. Inputs are \(n\times 3\) arrays of normalised homogeneous coordinates.

import numpy as np

def estimate_essential(x1, x2):
    """Linear 8-point + projection onto the essential space. x1, x2: (n,3), n >= 8."""
    A = np.einsum("ni,nj->nij", x2, x1).reshape(-1, 9)   # rows: kron(x2_i, x1_i)
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)                           # SVD 1
    E_hat = Vt[-1].reshape(3, 3)                          # null vector -> 3x3
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E_hat)                       # SVD 2
    return U @ np.diag([1.0, 1.0, 0.0]) @ Vt              # nearest essential matrix

def triangulate(P1, P2, a, b):
    M = np.stack([a[0]*P1[2] - P1[0], a[1]*P1[2] - P1[1],
                  b[0]*P2[2] - P2[0], b[1]*P2[2] - P2[1]])
    X = np.linalg.svd(M)[2][-1]                           # SVD 4
    return X[:3] / X[3]

def decompose_essential(E, x1, x2):
    """Return the (R, t) with the most points in front of both cameras; |t| = 1."""
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E)                           # SVD 3
    if np.linalg.det(U) < 0:  U  = -U
    if np.linalg.det(Vt) < 0: Vt = -Vt
    W = np.array([[0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]])
    P1 = np.hstack([np.eye(3), np.zeros((3, 1))])
    best = None
    for R in (U @ W @ Vt, U @ W.T @ Vt):
        for t in (U[:, 2], -U[:, 2]):
            P2 = np.hstack([R, t[:, None]])
            Xs = np.array([triangulate(P1, P2, a, b) for a, b in zip(x1, x2)])
            n_front = np.sum((Xs[:, 2] > 0) & ((Xs @ R.T + t)[:, 2] > 0))
            if best is None or n_front > best[0]:
                best = (n_front, R, t)
    return best[1], best[2]

In production you would rarely write this yourself. OpenCV wraps the robust version: cv2.findEssentialMat runs Nistér's 5-point solver inside RANSAC, and cv2.recoverPose performs exactly the decomposition and cheirality test above.

import cv2
E, inliers = cv2.findEssentialMat(pts1, pts2, K, method=cv2.RANSAC, prob=0.999, threshold=1.0)
n_good, R, t, mask = cv2.recoverPose(E, pts1, pts2, K, mask=inliers)   # pts in pixels, K = intrinsics

11Failure modes and practical notes

Where matching breaks

  • Textureless surfaces (white walls, sky, smooth road): every disparity costs the same. Active stereo adds texture by projecting an infrared dot pattern. Otherwise, smoothness priors such as SGM fill the gap with a guess.
  • Occlusions: pixels beside a depth edge are visible in one camera only. The left-right check detects them; it cannot recover them.
  • Repetitive patterns (fences, tiles, windows): several disparities fit equally well, which gives confident wrong answers. The uniqueness ratio test in SGBM rejects pixels whose best and second-best costs are too close.
  • Non-Lambertian surfaces: specular highlights and reflections move with the viewpoint, and transparent surfaces show two depths at once.
  • Radiometric differences: different exposure, gain or vignetting between the cameras. Use census or NCC costs, or match on gradients.

Where the geometry breaks

  • Calibration drift. Temperature changes, vibration and mounting stress shift the extrinsics by tiny angles. Because \(\delta Z \propto Z^2\), a small rotation error is a large depth bias at range. Online recalibration estimates \(E\) from live feature matches, with RANSAC to reject outliers, and corrects \(R\) and \(\mathbf t\). The baseline length is held at its calibrated value, since \(E\) cannot recover scale.
  • Synchronisation and rolling shutter. If the two exposures are not simultaneous, or rows are read at different times, motion creates false disparity. Hardware triggering and global-shutter sensors avoid it.
  • Degenerate motion for E. Pure rotation makes \(E\) vanish; use a homography instead. Planar scenes are degenerate for the 8-point algorithm, though not for Nistér's 5-point solver. Forward motion with small rotation is poorly conditioned: try "mostly forward" in the section 05 lab with high noise.
  • Sign hygiene. Always force \(\det U = \det V = +1\) before building \(R\). Otherwise you can produce a reflection with \(\det R = -1\) that still satisfies the epipolar constraint.
